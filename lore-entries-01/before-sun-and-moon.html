Before Sun and Moon

A restricted Enkanomiyan chronicle preserved as Volume II of the Byakuyakoku Collection, Before Sun and Moon recounts the Primordial One, the dragon Sovereigns, a second heavenly throne, and the history of the Dainichi Mikoshi.

A forbidden Enkanomiyan chronicle

Before Sun and Moon is a Quest Item recovered through the Enkanomiya World Quest Antigonus. After it is handed in during Collection of Dragons and Snakes, the same text becomes Volume II of the five-volume Byakuyakoku Collection. Its description calls the book forbidden to ordinary people and describes it as a mixture of fables and histories that reaches from the beginning of the world to the construction of the Dainichi Mikoshi.

The author is not named. The final entry identifies the writer only as a scribe of Tokoyo Ookami. The book is a crucial Enkanomiyan account, but its own description tells readers to expect both history and fable rather than a detached, complete chronicle.

The Primordial One and the dragon Sovereigns

In “When the Doves Held Branches,” the writer says that the eternal throne of the heavens came and the world was made anew. The Primordial One then battled seven dragon-lords of the old world, called the Seven Sovereigns. The account says the Primordial One created four shining Shades of itself, but does not give their names in this passage.

A following section, “On Phanes, or The Primordial One,” is deliberately less certain: the Primordial One “may have been Phanes.” It describes a winged, crowned, androgynous being born from an egg whose broken shell separates the universe from the world’s microcosm. The book offers this as a possibility, not a definitive equation.

Creation, covenant, and the age of abundance

The chronicle’s calendar says the Seven Sovereigns were vanquished after forty years, after which seven nations submitted to heaven. Four hundred years later, the Primordial One and one Shade made animals, plants, and eventually humans, described as the ancestors of the Enkanomiyan writer. The people then made a covenant with the Primordial One.

The entries “The Year of the Ark’s Opening” and “The Year of Jubilee” describe a world in which human happiness pleased the Primordial One, food and rain came when needed, the earth answered poverty with riches, and heaven answered melancholy with its voice. The one taboo was succumbing to temptation, though the text says the path to temptation had already been sealed. It does not name the modern nations or establish that every detail is a literal historical report.

The Second Who Came and the descent into darkness

Under “The Funerary Year,” the scribe records that a second throne of the heavens arrived and war began again. The heavens collapsed, the earth was torn apart, and the ancestors and their homeland fell into the place where they now live. The chronicle calls the era that followed an age of darkness.

It says the people of the Seven Sovereigns had taken refuge in the oceans and that the Dragonheirs of the Depths ruled this place. The ancestors fought them with lantern light. The writer does not describe the war’s combatants in full or witness its beginning; the surviving lines are a local account of its consequences in Byakuyakoku.

Parables of the sun and the lotus

Two named parables interrupt the calendar. In “The Parable of the Sun,” people mistake a torch for the sun after a sage describes daylight; whoever controls the flame can cast a shadow across the land. “The Parable of the Lethied Lotus” tells of people who eat flowers that make them forget their troubles, while a ship captain searches for a way back to the surface.

The text presents these as parables, not as a key that assigns every character to a known ruler. They deepen the book’s themes of limited knowledge, control, temptation, and the longing to return home.

Kairos, the Ruler of Time

In the Third Year of Darkness, the scribe says the people recognized one figure as the “Ruler of Time,” the one who had not forsaken them. The writer calls her Kairos and says her hidden name will be written only once, in reverse: “Htoratsi.” Reversing those letters gives Istaroth.

This is the book’s own identification puzzle. It does not list every Shade’s name or describe a direct conversation with the Ruler of Time. Later records about Istaroth and Tokoyo Ookami can be compared with it, but the passage should not be expanded into a claim that all events in Enkanomiya were personally directed by her.

Abrax and the first year of Sun and Moon

Under the Year of Blindness, the chronicle says the sage Abrax gained wisdom and revealed a light-bringing miracle. He led the construction of Helios. When the device was complete, the Whitenight began and the calendar reset to the First Year of Sun and Moon. In the collection’s companion volume, Helios is associated with the Dainichi Mikoshi.

The next year, the ancestors sought a route back to the surface. The book says the Primordial One had laid down a ban against their return. The scribe then reasons, “In that case,” the Primordial One must have defeated the Second Who Came. That is the writer’s inference from the restriction, not a separately narrated or witnessed outcome of the war. The same entry says Abrax was imprisoned by order of the Sunchild.

The parable of the tree and the scribe’s ending

“The Parable of the Tree” depicts a king who needs a spirit-tree cut down, a gardener who obeys, and a priest who instructs him to plant its branches. The text identifies the king with the Primordial One and the priest with Tokoyo Ookami. A new tree grows at once, continuing the former spirit. The closing lines call this the work of the God of Moments, who can take seeds from one moment into the past and future. The text leaves the parable’s full referent unexplained.

The chronicle ends in the tenth year of Sun and Moon. The scribe says Abrax is gone and that the events before the sun and moon have been sufficiently recorded. They insist on writing what happened as it happened, then hear armor outside and stop. The book never reveals who entered or what happened to the writer.

The ban, Orobashi, and the book’s recovery

The quests around the book add a later history. In Collection of Dragons and Snakes, Ema calls it a restricted chronicle and says Watatsumi Omikami ordered it banned. She says it was stolen and asks the Traveler to investigate three suspects connected with Khaenri’ah. Antigonus recounts a separate attempt by Khaenri’ah envoys to seize the book, which Antei says he prevented. These events do not make Khaenri’ah its author.

In Erebos’ Secret, Eboshi says Watatsumi Omikami encountered a truth older than itself and was forced by heaven to sacrifice itself so the people could live under the rule of the Seven. She does not specify which line in the chronicle was that truth or explain the exact terms of the sacrifice. Separately, Enjou’s search during From Dusk to Dawn in Byakuyakoku shows that the Abyss Order also wanted the book; its interest is not proof that the book validates the Order’s conclusions.

How to read its historical claims

The book is one of the game’s most important surviving cosmological records, but it is still an Enkanomiyan scribe’s account. It combines chronology, theological language, parables, and the writer’s deductions. That distinction matters for the line that the Primordial One may have been Phanes and for the claim that the first throne defeated the Second Who Came.

It does not identify the Second Who Came, name all four Shades, explain the full cause of the ancient war, or prove that every parable is a disguised event. Its ellipses also mark gaps in the surviving sequence. Later quest dialogue adds context about Enkanomiya, Orobashi, and the book’s suppression; open questions should remain open where neither the chronicle nor those quests supply an answer.
