General History of Snezhnaya

Y. S. Klyucheva’s two-volume Snezhnayan history pairs uncertain Hyperborean legends with accounts of Monomakh, the fae kindreds, and Tsaritsa Anastasya.

Book record and the Tsaritsa’s preface

General History of Snezhnaya is a two-volume collection credited to Y. S. Klyucheva. The preface dedicates it to Anastasya Feodorovna and says the work will cover legends from the Age of Polar Night, Tsar Monomakh’s campaigns, and the deeds of Tsaritsa Anastasya. Its language repeatedly praises the reigning Tsaritsa and presents the author as her grateful subject, making the book’s courtly point of view explicit.

That framing matters throughout the collection. It treats virtue and mercy as the standards by which rulers should be judged, while describing political history through the lens of the Pale Star court. The text can preserve valuable names, traditions, and competing reports without functioning as a neutral record.

Two traditions about Hyperborea

The first volume, titled “Legends of Hyperborea,” describes traditions that Snezhnaya was once a warm, fertile land. One account, attributed to Grand Duke P. N. Kostomarov, says that a virtuous people lived in an eternal spring until their descendants lost their nobility and winter came. A second, attributed to the Songbook of Saint Strivozha, tells of a King of Light, a Mother of Night, seven Preexistent Gods, and twelve Preexistent Men. In that version, the King of Light destroys Hyperborea after day and night begin to alternate.

The book warns that the source writers lived far from the age they describe and that their accounts may be history or imagination. It then calls these accounts “strange fables” before introducing Monomakh as true history. That is the narrator’s division between legend and history; the surviving text does not independently verify every claim that follows.

Yalyane Morevna and Monomakh’s youth

The surviving account of Monomakh’s travels presents Yalyane Morevna, the Knyazhna of Blood, as a teacher who showed him the spear and taught him mercy. At the same time, it says that she appears only rarely in annals it calls authentic and that most accounts about her come from folk legend and song. She is portrayed both as a violent raider of fae kindreds and as a protector of humans who faced monsters.

The chronicler does not smooth away those differences. It notes that The Davograd Chronicles condemn Yalyane in one respect while omitting the alleged spoils of her raids, then says the true account is difficult to establish. This makes her an important figure in Monomakh’s remembered education, but not one whose complete biography can be reconstructed from these passages.

Filipov, Nepla, and the idea of mercy

Monomakh later meets Filipov Vseslavyevich, a shape-shifter who becomes his counselor and the first commander of the Druzhna. Their contest is told as a chain of increasingly extravagant transformations and mountain throws. The author calls one version the most credible, but later says such episodes cannot be verified; the scene is best read as heroic legend attached to a figure the chronicles consistently associate with cunning and victory.

The account then introduces Nepla Korolevitsna, a human warrior guarding her clan. After Filipov defeats her at the cost of an eye, Monomakh refuses the customary choice of killing or enslaving the defeated clan and instead directs them to settle where Port Belovodye would later stand. The book connects this decision to the lesson he says he learned from Yalyane. Filipov marries Nepla, whose human lifespan is brief beside his, and the text later links grief over her death to his susceptibility to a deceiver wearing her likeness.

Campaigns and rival chronicles

Later surviving passages describe rival fae kindreds, shifts in allegiance, and Monomakh’s conflicts with King Miscislav and the Volkodlak Alliance. They also discuss the Vila, the Water Imps, and the consequences of Vaselisa Vodyanovna leaving Miscislav’s coalition. The narrative sometimes distinguishes what it believes can be established from what it cannot determine, including the motives behind Vaselisa Strivozha’s actions.

The source trail is deliberately plural: the book invokes the Volkhov Chronicles, the Belovodye Annals, The Davograd Chronicles, and A History of the Snezhnyi Expedition. Those accounts do not always agree, and some episodes are separated by ellipses that mark missing text. The surviving fragments therefore should not be assembled into a seamless campaign chronology.

What this history establishes—and leaves open

The collection records Snezhnayan traditions about Hyperborea, Monomakh’s rise, the fae kindreds, and the later struggle with Miscislav. It provides named sources and preserves competing memories of Yalyane, Filipov, Nepla, and the war. Its political themes include rule, loyalty, the treatment of defeated peoples, and the difference between force and mercy.

Its preface is openly flattering to the Tsaritsa, its historical narration is selective, and some episodes are explicitly legendary or incomplete. A Brief History of the Pale Starborn and the three General History supplements add other versions of the Pale Star War. Where those records conflict, the game leaves the disagreement visible; this book alone does not settle a single definitive account.
