The Headless Coquitao

This two-volume Masters of the Night-Wind scroll tells of Coquitao’s pact, betrayal, headless vengeance, and the later inheritance of his club Makana, while warning that its claimed age is hard to verify.

A Weavers’ Scroll with an Uncertain Age

The Headless Coquitao is a two-volume woven scroll attributed to the Masters of the Night-Wind. Its own description says the stories are older than the tribe but that this is difficult to verify. Volume I frames stories as tapestries woven from the night’s domain and Mictlan, then presents the warrior Coquitao and his stone club Makana.

The scroll is important as a record of the tribe’s folklore and storytelling frame. Its description warns against treating its claimed antiquity as established historical fact.

Volume I: The Pact and Betrayal

Coquitao makes a pact with a deity of the dog days, mortgaging his fate to the Kame Twins. With Makana, he is charged with bringing death to those who have forgotten it and returning lost souls to Mictlan. After the violence ends, the deity refuses to return his soul; the Kame Twins direct Nagual, a shapeshifter, to behead him.

The story uses several titles rather than a complete roster of named historical figures: She-With-The-Broken-Visage, a mistress of heavenly stars, a jade-skirted master, the Night-Lord, and the Lord of the Night. Their relationships are part of the scroll’s mythic narrative, not a guide to assigning every title to a known god.

Volume II: Vengeance and Makana’s Legacy

The mistress of heavenly stars sends Tzitzimimeh to guide the headless Coquitao toward revenge. He finds Nagual in a scorched land and kills him, but the book says Coquitao’s spirit has already become one with the earth; his body continues to wander with a lingering rage.

The ending says many later heroes inherit Makana during times of trouble and names Och-Kan as one of them, leaving his story for another telling. The scroll does not identify its other figures with named characters or prove that its tale records an actual sequence of Natlan’s history.

Reading the Night and Moon Imagery

Mictlan, blind weavers, moonlight, Tzitzimimeh, and the three moons’ death give the scroll a layered cosmic atmosphere. These are images within the Masters of the Night-Wind’s story. The shared title “Lord of the Night” can be compared with Natlan’s later lore, but the scroll does not name Yohualtecuhtli or assign a confirmed identity to every deity it describes.
