Written Signs Across Teyvat

Teyvat displays recurring and region-specific writing systems, some of which players can decode. Their visual similarities do not prove a single universal language or one ancient civilization behind every inscription.

Visible Lettering Is Not One Universal Cipher

The game places many kinds of lettering on signs, books, ruins, and objects. ‘Teyvat Script’ is a community name for a recurring alphabetic symbol set seen across parts of the open world. Players have mapped many of its glyphs to Latin letters, which can make some short signs readable, but the game has not published an authoritative grammar or a complete language manual.

Players also use regional labels such as Inazuman, Sumeru, Deshret, Fontaine, and Snezhnayan script for distinct visual forms. Some appear to adapt familiar glyphs or encode English and regionally inspired words. These names describe observed writing; they do not prove that every nation uses a separate spoken language, or that every mark in the environment decodes into a deliberate message.

Some Inscriptions Use Other Systems

The Ishine Script is associated with the priestly families of old Tsurumi Island and with names used there. The game also shows Latin-based inscriptions in contexts associated with ancient ruins, alchemy, gods, and the Abyss; players often call this the ‘Abyss Language.’ Similar-looking signs across distant sites can invite a shared-history theory, but appearance alone does not establish that one civilization created every inscription.

Hilichurlian is a constructed language studied in quests and in Ella Musk’s handbook; it is not simply another alphabet for the same common writing. Other visual systems, including the limited Adeptus markings, have their own contexts. Keep language, writing system, decorative font, and cipher as separate terms.

Treat Decoding as a Reading Aid

Community-deciphered alphabets can help read environmental details, but transcription can be uncertain and some sequences are decorative, inconsistent, or not legible enough to translate. A decoded line can suggest where to look next; it does not automatically prove a historical theory about the world.

This guide separates what is visible in the game from community-built decoding tables and from larger interpretations about an ancient unified civilization. Use the original scene, its quest or item context, and the limits of the decoding before treating a sign as canon text.
